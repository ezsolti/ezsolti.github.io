How this tuner works

1. Getting the sound

The browser's Web Audio API streams the microphone. The phone's echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain control are switched off: they're built for speech and can smear or modulate a pitch. An 80 Hz high-pass filter removes rumble and handling noise. Every screen frame (~60×/s) the tuner takes the latest N = 4096 samples, about 85 ms at 48 kHz. That's enough for 20+ periods of the lowest string (C4, 3.8 ms per period) while still responding quickly.

2. Why not simply pick the biggest FFT peak?

The obvious approach is to FFT the signal and take the frequency of the highest bin. That has two problems:

  • Overtones. A plucked string vibrates at f, 2f, 3f… at once, and on a nylon uke string the 2nd or 3rd harmonic is often louder than the fundamental. The highest peak is then an octave or a twelfth above the note.
  • Resolution. FFT bins are fs/N apart: 11.7 Hz for 4096 samples. Near C4, one cent is only about 0.15 Hz, so you'd need a window several seconds long to resolve it directly.

So the tuner asks a different question: after what delay does the waveform repeat itself? The period is shared by every harmonic, and it can be measured very precisely.

3. Autocorrelation, computed with the FFT

The autocorrelation compares the signal with a copy of itself shifted by a lag τ:

r(τ) = Σj xj · xj+τ

It peaks when τ equals the period, and at multiples of it. Computing it directly costs N² operations. The Wiener–Khinchin theorem gives a faster route, because the autocorrelation is the inverse Fourier transform of the power spectrum:

r = IFFT( |FFT(x)|² )

That's two FFTs, O(N log N). This is where the FFT is used. The signal is zero-padded to 2N = 8192 before transforming. Without padding the FFT treats the window as periodic, so the correlation would wrap around: the end of the buffer would be compared with its beginning. Since |FFT(x)|² is real and symmetric, a second forward FFT gives the same result as the inverse, up to a factor of 2N, so the code reuses one routine.

The mean is subtracted first so a DC offset doesn't add a constant to every lag.

4. Normalising it (McLeod's NSDF)

Raw autocorrelation shrinks at larger lags, simply because fewer samples overlap, and it depends on loudness. The normalised square difference function (Philip McLeod, 2005) fixes both:

n(τ) = 2·r(τ) / m(τ),   m(τ) = Σj=0N−1−τ (xj² + xj+τ²)

n(τ) lies between −1 and +1, and +1 means a perfect repeat at that lag. m(τ) is updated incrementally, subtracting two squared samples per step, so it's essentially free.

5. Picking the period

  1. Skip the initial positive lobe around τ = 0 (any signal resembles itself at tiny shifts).
  2. In each following positive region, keep its highest point: a candidate period.
  3. If the best candidate is below 0.7, the sound isn't periodic enough: it's noise, speech or silence, and nothing is shown. A level gate also ignores very quiet input.
  4. Candidates scoring at least 85% of the best are kept. Real periods at τ0, 2τ0, 3τ0… all score high, so this is where octave errors could still happen. To prevent that, the tuner picks the candidate closest to the string you selected. A string that's a few semitones off still lands far nearer its true period than its double.

6. Sub-sample precision

The lag is a whole number of samples, and at 440 Hz one sample step is about 16 cents. So a parabola is fitted through the peak and its two neighbours, and its vertex is used:

τ* = τ + ½ · (a − c) / (a − 2b + c)

where a, b, c are n(τ−1), n(τ), n(τ+1). On clean test tones this lands within a fraction of a cent. The frequency is then f = fs / τ*.

7. From frequency to needle

Pitch is compared in cents (100 cents = 1 semitone), a logarithmic measure that means the same at any pitch:

cents = 1200 · log₂( f / ftarget )

The needle covers ±50 cents across 100°. Anything beyond that holds it at the end stop. Within ±5 cents counts as in tune (green). To steady the display, the reading is the median of the last 5 estimates, which throws out one-off glitches, and the needle eases toward it rather than jumping.

8. Why a fresh pluck often reads sharp

Plucking stretches the string, which briefly raises its tension and pitch. The pitch then drops to its resting value within a fraction of a second, and harder plucks start sharper. Add the noisy "click" of the attack and the smoothing delay, and the needle settles a moment after the pluck. Trust the reading once it stops moving, and pluck gently.

Settings in the code

STRINGS (tuning), SCALE_CENTS (needle range), IN_TUNE (green tolerance) and RMS_MIN (silence gate) are at the top of the script.
